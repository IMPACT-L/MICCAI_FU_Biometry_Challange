\begin{abstract}
\noindent Accurate ultrasound biometry depends on precise landmark localization across heterogeneous anatomies, acquisition protocols, and devices. We present a unified multi-task framework that combines a pretrained DINOv3 encoder with task-specific feature pyramids, context-local task adaptation, high-resolution heatmaps, and learned sub-pixel offset refinement. The model preserves global anatomical context while maintaining the local precision required for biometric measurements. We study backbone scaling, feature sharing, decoder specialization, heatmap resolution, and validation design under one training pipeline. The selected DINOv3 ViT-B model with task-specific feature aggregation and 128\,$\times$\,128 heatmaps achieves 5.64-pixel average MRE on a released-annotation consistency evaluation across nine task subsets. A 256\,$\times$\,256 heatmap ablation reduces this consistency error to 5.53 pixels, but is not selected because its held-out checkpoint-selection score is worse. These results support high-resolution coarse-to-fine multi-task modeling for robust ultrasound biometry.

\keywords{Ultrasound Biometry \and Multi-Task Learning \and DINOv3 \and Landmark Localization \and Vision Transformers}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
This paper addresses the problem of building a single ultrasound landmark localization model that remains accurate across heterogeneous anatomies, acquisition protocols, and devices while still producing clinically meaningful biometric measurements. In the Foundation Model for Ultrasound Biometry benchmark, one submission must process fetal, intrapartum, and adult cardiac ultrasound with substantially different appearance and geometry. Some subsets require only two endpoints, whereas others require dense multi-landmark configurations over a wide field of view. This setting makes anatomy-specific pipelines inconvenient and motivates a unified model that can share representation learning while still adapting to task-specific landmark structure.

Our goal in this work is not only to reduce landmark error on a local validation split, but to develop a single-model pipeline that better matches the official challenge objective. The benchmark ranking combines normalized mean radial error (MRE) and normalized parameter prediction error with equal weight, so good performance requires both precise landmark localization and geometrically consistent predictions for downstream measurements. During development, this exposed a central difficulty: models that looked strong under one local selection criterion did not always remain strong under stricter validation settings. The paper therefore focuses on the model and training decisions that were most relevant to this gap.

We build the study around a unified DINOv3-based architecture with challenge-compliant preprocessing, task-conditioned heatmap prediction, soft-argmax coordinate decoding, learned offset refinement, and measurement-aware analysis. The system remains a single model rather than an ensemble or a collection of anatomy-specific networks. Our contributions are a common landmark pipeline for all nine subsets, a coarse-to-fine DINOv3 architecture with task-specific feature aggregation and high-resolution offsets, and an empirical analysis of backbone scaling, FPN sharing, decoder specialization, paired-landmark canonicalization, heatmap resolution, and validation design.

\section{Related Work}
Automated ultrasound biometry has often been studied through anatomy-specific pipelines for fetal head, abdomen, long-bone, intrapartum, and cardiac measurements~\cite{baumgartner2017sononet,isuog2018intrapartum,isuog2019biometry,isuog2022preterm,isuog2023cardiac}. For the setting studied in this paper, however, the key problem is not only anatomy recognition but precise landmark localization across multiple domains. Landmark-based formulations are especially attractive because the same predicted points can be evaluated geometrically and converted into downstream biometric measurements~\cite{payer2016regressing,payer2019integrating}. This makes landmark localization a natural common representation for a benchmark that mixes line-like, diameter-like, and dense multi-point tasks.

Our method is most directly related to heatmap-based keypoint localization. Stacked hourglass networks, HRNet, and CenterNet-style formulations showed that dense spatial supervision remains highly effective for accurate point prediction~\cite{newell2016hourglass,sun2019hrnet,zhou2019objects}. Structured landmark models further indicate that explicit spatial reasoning helps when appearance cues are weak or ambiguous~\cite{payer2019integrating}. These observations are particularly relevant in ultrasound, where speckle, acoustic shadowing, and low-contrast boundaries can make direct coordinate regression unstable.

The broader medical-image literature has also shown the value of dense prediction backbones and foundation-style encoders. U-Net and nnU-Net established strong fully convolutional baselines for biomedical dense prediction~\cite{ronneberger2015unet,isensee2021nnu}, while transformer-based medical architectures such as TransUNet and UNETR demonstrated that global context can improve medical image representation learning~\cite{chen2021transunet,hatamizadeh2022unetr}. Recent general and medical foundation models, including SAM and MedSAM, further illustrate the importance of transferable spatial representations~\cite{kirillov2023sam,ma2024medsam}. Our work differs from these segmentation-oriented methods by using landmark heatmaps and coordinate decoding, but it follows the same motivation of combining transferable representations with task-specific spatial prediction.

The shared encoder in our model is motivated by recent self-supervised vision transformers. ViT, Swin Transformer, MAE, BYOL, DINO, DINOv2, and DINOv3 have shown that large-scale pretrained representations transfer effectively across tasks and domains~\cite{dosovitskiy2021vit,liu2021swin,he2022mae,grill2020byol,caron2021dino,oquab2024dinov2,simeoni2025dinov3}. Rather than training separate models for each subset, we use this line of work to justify a shared representation backbone for all nine tasks and then study how much specialization is still needed in the neck and decoder. The implementation uses PyTorch Image Models for pretrained backbone integration~\cite{timm}.

Our training design is also connected to multi-task learning. Hard parameter sharing, soft parameter sharing, cross-stitch style feature exchange, and uncertainty-based weighting have all been proposed to balance related but non-identical objectives~\cite{caruana1997,ruder2017overview,misra2016crossstitch,kendall2018multitask}. The Foundation Model for Ultrasound Biometry setting is a concrete example of this trade-off: all tasks come from the same imaging modality, but the landmark count, spatial layout, and measurement semantics differ substantially. This motivates our comparison of shared backbones with task-specific feature aggregation and task-conditioned decoding in a single challenge-ready model.

\section{Method}
\subsection{Problem formulation}
Each training image belongs to one of nine tasks:
\texttt{A4C}, \texttt{AOP}, \texttt{FA}, \texttt{FUGC}, \texttt{HC}, \texttt{IVC}, \texttt{PLAX}, \texttt{PSAX}, or \texttt{fetal\_femur}. The number of landmarks ranges from two to twenty-two depending on the anatomy. We cast all tasks into a common heatmap-based landmark regression framework. Task identity determines the number of active landmarks, the output head, and any optional auxiliary losses, while the rest of the training pipeline remains shared.

\subsection{Model overview}
Our unified framework uses a shared pretrained DINOv3 encoder, followed by either a shared or task-specific feature pyramid neck, a task-conditioned heatmap head for each dataset subset, differentiable soft-argmax decoding for coordinate extraction, and learned offset refinement for sub-pixel localization. The main design principle is coarse-to-fine prediction. Deep encoder features provide task-relevant global context, the neck recovers spatial detail, the heatmap decoder produces a coarse landmark probability field, and the offset branch refines the final coordinate estimate. Figure~\ref{fig:overview} summarizes the unified multi-task setup.

\subsection{Shared backbone and feature neck}
Our main model family uses a pretrained DINOv3 visual transformer as the shared encoder. We initially used a ViT-B backbone and later added support for \texttt{vit\_large\_patch16\_dinov3}. The final selected architecture uses DINOv3 ViT-B because it provided the best balance between accuracy, stability, and training cost in the single-model setting. Training uses mixed precision and gradient accumulation where needed.

On top of the encoder, we use either a shared FPN or a task-specific FPN. The task-specific variant consistently performed better in our experiments. We attribute this to the strong geometric mismatch between challenge subsets: dense cardiac landmarking, compact ring-like structures, and short endpoint tasks require different balances between semantic context and local detail.

\subsection{Task-specific decoding}
We investigated several decoder families, including a uniform deep heatmap head shared across all tasks except for output dimensionality, geometry-aware variants for line-like, compact, dense, and femur-like structures, fully dedicated per-task decoders, and soft-sharing adapters that partially separate related task groups. The final model combines context-local task adaptation with specialized decoders for the most geometry-sensitive subsets. The \texttt{A4C} head uses a cardiac-graph decoder to preserve multi-landmark structure, \texttt{HC} uses a refined compact-structure decoder, \texttt{IVC} uses a two-point band-oriented decoder, and \texttt{FUGC} uses a short-segment refinement head. The remaining tasks use high-resolution offset heatmap decoders. This design keeps the shared encoder stable while giving difficult geometries enough capacity for local refinement.

\subsection{Training objectives}
The final model is trained with a base landmark loss that combines dense heatmap supervision and direct coordinate supervision:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{base}} =
\mathcal{L}_{\text{heatmap}} + 0.2 \mathcal{L}_{\text{coord}}.
\end{equation}

Here, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{heatmap}}$ supervises the predicted landmark heatmaps at the output resolution, while $\mathcal{L}_{\text{coord}}$ penalizes the difference between decoded landmark coordinates and the reference coordinates in image space. This combination gives the model both dense spatial guidance and direct point-level refinement. In the final configuration, the heatmap term provides the main localization signal, the coordinate term sharpens the final landmark positions, and learned offset heads further correct the discretization error introduced by heatmap decoding.

For geometry-sensitive subsets, the training framework also supports task-aware auxiliary supervision based on clinically meaningful structures such as short segments and long-bone shafts. This allows the optimization to remain aligned with the measurement geometry of the underlying anatomy while preserving a single unified landmark-learning objective across all tasks.

\subsection{Data pipeline}
The released datasets were standardized into a common CSV schema to support unified loading, preprocessing, and submission generation. Images are resized with aspect-ratio-preserving letterboxing so that coordinate transforms remain consistent across training, evaluation, and JSON export.

A practically important issue was task-specific ordering ambiguity for paired landmarks. To avoid supervision noise in two-point and paired-diameter tasks, we introduced canonical pair ordering for subsets such as \texttt{FUGC}, \texttt{IVC}, \texttt{AOP}, \texttt{FA}, \texttt{HC}, \texttt{PSAX}, and \texttt{fetal\_femur}. This stabilizes both optimization and derived measurement consistency.

\section{Experiments and Results}
\subsection{Dataset and evaluation protocol}
All experiments use the public Foundation Model for Ultrasound Biometry release and its nine task subsets~\cite{mwm2026,fubiometrychallenge,fubiometrybaseline}. The anonymized data are distributed for non-commercial research under the organizer-provided CC BY-NC license, with ethics approvals reported by the organizers. Local development is performed on train/validation splits derived from the released annotations. The released-annotation consistency evaluation contains 6768 images: 108 \texttt{A4C}, 4000 \texttt{AOP}, 500 \texttt{FA}, 260 \texttt{FUGC}, 999 \texttt{HC}, 38 \texttt{IVC}, 87 \texttt{PLAX}, 49 \texttt{PSAX}, and 727 \texttt{fetal\_femur} samples. Because hidden-test labels are unavailable, we distinguish between held-out checkpoint-selection metrics and a full released-annotation consistency evaluation used to verify the final model and submission pipeline. The official evaluation combines landmark MRE and clinically derived parameter error with normalized aggregation. To better reflect this setting, we additionally introduced grouped validation splits and weighted checkpoint selection that emphasize historically difficult local subsets.

\subsection{Implementation details}
Images are resized to 512\,$\times$\,512 with letterboxing. The final selected model predicts heatmaps at 128\,$\times$\,128 resolution and uses learned offset heads to reduce quantization error in the final coordinates. Models are implemented in PyTorch, use pretrained backbones through PyTorch Image Models, and are optimized with AdamW and cosine learning-rate decay. We use mixed precision, exponential moving average checkpoints, and early stopping. Training and evaluation were performed on NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs with 24\,GB memory. For larger backbones, gradient accumulation is used to maintain an effective batch size without exceeding GPU memory.

The selected model in the current study is the offset-refined DINOv3 ViT-B model using task-specific FPNs, context-local adapters, and 128\,$\times$\,128 heatmaps. It achieved 5.64 average released-annotation MRE, with per-task errors ranging from 1.71 on \texttt{FUGC} to 11.44 on \texttt{IVC}. On the held-out checkpoint-selection split, the average MRE was 15.62 pixels, the average derived-measurement MAE was 10.90 pixels, and the normalized combined score was 0.329. A 256\,$\times$\,256 heatmap stress test reduced the released-annotation MRE to 5.53 pixels, but its held-out normalized combined score worsened to 0.336. We therefore select the 128-resolution model as the more robust operating point. Relative to the earlier project line, the final architecture provides a substantial reduction in local error, indicating the benefit of pretrained transformer features, task-specific feature aggregation, higher heatmap resolution, and learned offset refinement.

\subsection{Ablation findings}
The ablation study showed several consistent patterns. Task-specific FPNs were better than a shared FPN across multiple runs and backbone sizes. Backbone scaling improved early baselines, but the largest gain came from increasing heatmap resolution from 64\,$\times$\,64 to 128\,$\times$\,128 and adding learned offset refinement. Increasing resolution further to 256\,$\times$\,256 improved released-annotation consistency but not held-out selection quality. Decoder quality also mattered more than naive refinement depth: the final architecture uses specialized decoders only where geometry demands it and otherwise relies on stable high-resolution offset heads.

\section{Discussion}
The main remaining gap is robust cross-domain generalization under scanner, device, and acquisition shifts. The final offset-refined model reduced local landmark error substantially, but sensitivity to validation design indicates that row-level splits can underestimate generalization difficulty. We therefore use grouped validation, ultrasound-style augmentation, and weighted checkpoint selection for difficult subsets such as \texttt{IVC}, \texttt{A4C}, and \texttt{HC}.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
Local validation remains an approximation of full cross-domain generalization, and challenge-normalized measurement quality is only partially reflected by our current local selection metric. The reported 5.64-pixel result is a released-annotation consistency evaluation rather than a hidden-test estimate. Future work should emphasize stronger domain generalization, measurement-aware validation, and principled feature-sharing or task-balancing mechanisms~\cite{caruana1997,kendall2018multitask,misra2016crossstitch}.

\section{Conclusion}
We presented a unified coarse-to-fine ultrasound biometry framework based on DINOv3 features, task-specific feature pyramids, context-local adaptation, high-resolution heatmaps, and offset refinement. The selected DINOv3 ViT-B model achieved 5.64 average MRE on the released-annotation consistency evaluation. Strong pretrained representations are necessary but not sufficient; high-resolution localization, constrained task specialization, and domain-aware validation are also central to robust ultrasound biometry.
